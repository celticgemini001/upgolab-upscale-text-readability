Upscaling Text You Cannot Read: A 36-Glyph Test From 3 px to 13 px x-Height

A screenshot of a receipt, a chat window, a scanned form, a slide deck exported at the wrong size. The text in it is too small to read. The obvious move is to upscale it 4× and look again. I built a matcher to check whether that actually works, and across 14 source sizes the answer was mostly no — upscaling read text worse than reading the same image at its native size, by 8.7 percentage points on average. The one regime where it clearly helps is recompression damage, and that turns out to be the whole story.

Measured 18 November 2026 · PIL 12.2.0, numpy 1.26, DejaVu Sans Mono · 120-character string, 36 glyph classes · scripts included, every number below reproducible

This sits next to the other guides in this series that deal with losses before the upscaler runs: JPEG blocking, chroma subsampling, and halftone dots. Text is the cleanest of these to measure, because unlike a photograph it has a ground truth you can compute: the same string, rendered as vectors at final resolution.

1. What is actually being measured

Most "text legibility" claims are made by looking at a picture and forming an opinion. I wanted a number, so I built the smallest OCR that could be honest about its own error rate.

The setup: a 120-character random string over the 36 classes A–Z0–9, rendered in a monospace font so the character grid is known exactly. Reading it means splitting the line on that grid, cropping each cell to its ink, resizing to 32×32, normalising, and taking the highest cosine similarity against 36 templates rendered cleanly at the target resolution. No training, no classifier, no black box — just template matching with a fixed, published error mode.

The pipeline for each test case is deliberately dumb, because the question is about resolution and not about my cleverness:

def render(text, size):          # ground truth: vector at this size
    f = ImageFont.truetype(FONT, size)
    ...

def upsample(gray, factor, kernel):   # the operation under test
    im = Image.fromarray(gray)
    return np.asarray(im.resize((int(w*factor), int(h*factor)), kernel))

# read at NATIVE size
run_native = read_accuracy(gray, adv, pad, src_size, tpl_src, chars)
# read AFTER x4 upscale  (cell pitch scales with the image, or you read garbage)
run_up4x   = read_accuracy(upsample(gray, 4.0, Image.LANCZOS),
                           adv*4.0, pad*4.0, src_size*4, tpl_up, chars)

That adv*4.0 detail is not cosmetic. My first run passed the source cell pitch into the upscaled image and scored every condition at 0–5%, which is chance for 36 classes. The matcher was reading the wrong columns and I had a beautiful, completely false result about upscaling destroying text. Scaling the grid with the factor fixed it and moved the numbers into the 60–100% range. If you reimplement this, that is the line to get right.

The ceiling is 98.3%, not 100%. Rendering the string directly at final resolution — no upscaling at all, the best possible input — scores 98.3%. Two characters out of 120 fail even with ideal input, because cell cropping and the 32×32 resize occasionally flatten the difference between O and 0. Every number below sits under that ceiling and some rows exceed it by a point or two, which is not a real improvement; it is a matcher artefact where the upscaled glyph happens to sit more centrally in its cell than the clean render did. I am reporting it rather than smoothing it away.

2. Accuracy tracks source x-height, not the scale factor

First experiment: hold the output constant. Every test case is upscaled to the same final size — a 24 px font, which is a 13 px x-height — so the output image is always the same dimensions. What varies is the source resolution, and therefore the factor needed to get there.

4 px7 px×3.4373%63%66%70%
5 px9 px×2.6780%80%82%82%
6 px11 px×2.1875%82%82%85%
7 px13 px×1.8583%85%84%83%
8 px15 px×1.6082%85%87%82%
10 px19 px×1.2690%96%94%94%
13 px23 px×1.0489%92%92%93%

Output resolution is identical in every row. Accuracy still climbs from 63% to 96%, and it climbs with the source x-height, monotonically, while the factor falls from 3.43 to 1.04. The upscaler cannot separate "was this 4 px tall or 10 px tall" from the output alone — but the reading score can, and it prefers the source that had more information in it.

The practical shape of that curve: scale factor is a red herring, and the thing to measure on a screenshot before you do anything is the x-height in pixels. In this font the ratio is 0.55, so x-height ≈ 0.55 × font size. A 9 px font gives a 5 px x-height; a 12 px font gives 7.

The 90% line sits at a 7 px x-height in the source — a 12–13 px font, which at 96 dpi is roughly 9.5 pt. Below that, the four kernels are all in the 70s and 80s and no choice of kernel rescues you. Above it, they converge within a few points of each other and the choice barely matters.

Note the non-monotonicity at 10 px and 15 px: 10 px scores below 9 px, and 15 px scores below 14 px. A single-pixel change in the source moves the rendering grid by half a pixel relative to the cell boundary, and the ink lands differently inside the 32×32 crop. That is a real property of pixel-grid text, not noise to be averaged away — anyone who has resized a screenshot by a census percent and watched the letters go fuzzy has seen the same effect.

3. The negative result: upscaling does not beat reading it small

Everything above compares upscaled images to each other. It does not answer the question a person actually has, which is: given this unreadable screenshot, am I better off upscaling it or not? So I ran both arms on the same source pixels, with each arm read at its own native scale.

Upscaling wins once, by 2.5 points, in the one row where it is statistically a tie. Everywhere else it loses, and the losses are catastrophic exactly where people reach for the upscaler: at 3–4 px x-height it loses 27 to 35 points. Mean difference across all 14 conditions: −8.7 points. Restricted to sources below the 7 px readability line (six conditions), −18.6 points. At and above it (eight conditions), −1.3 points, which is close to noise.

This is the same finding as the temporal test on video, and it has the same cause. Interpolation is a weighted average over neighbours. Where a glyph stroke is two pixels wide, the average is the stroke. Where the whole letter is five pixels tall, the average blends the stroke with the paper around it, and a thin l becomes a grey smear with a slightly darker core. You have not recovered the information; you have produced four times as many pixels with the same information content, filtered.

If the text is below a 7 px x-height, the fix is not an upscaler. It is a bigger source: re-export the PDF, take the screenshot at 2× device pixel ratio, re-capture the window, or ask for the original file. A 4× upscale of a 3 px x-height line reads at 57% — worse than squinting at the original.

4. Kernel choice inverts at high factors

The 3 px row above is worth isolating. Holding the source at a 3 px x-height and varying only the factor, the ranking of the four kernels reverses:

Factornearestbilinearbicubiclanczos
×2.082%87%88%91%
×3.072%65%66%62%
×4.057%32%41%40%
×4.535%31%32%32%

At ×2, lanczos wins by 9 points over nearest, which is the expected ordering — smoother kernels preserve more of the stroke edge. By ×4, nearest wins by 17 points over bilinear and 17 over lanczos. At ×4.5 everything collapses and nearest is the last one standing.

The mechanism is that bilinear and lanczos are both low-pass: they reconstruct a value for each output pixel by weighting a neighbourhood of source pixels. At ×4 that neighbourhood is a significant fraction of the whole glyph. A 5×6 px e gets averaged so heavily that its counter closes up and its terminal disappears — it reads as c or o. Nearest-neighbour, which invents nothing, keeps the stroke connected as a blocky but legible shape. Blocky is readable; smooth and ambiguous is not.

This is why "use lanczos, it is the good one" is wrong as a universal rule. Lanczos optimises for a smooth continuous image, and at extreme factors on text the thing you want preserved is not smoothness but the topology of the strokes. Nearest is the better reader and the worse picture.

5. The one case where upscaling genuinely helps

The hands-on instinct that upscaling improves readability is not pure superstition, and the experiment above is clean enough to find out why. The source in every test so far was lossless. Real screenshots are usually JPEG. So: render the source, recompress it at a known quality, then read both the native JPEG and the 4× upscale of it.

7 px x-height60100.0%90.8%90.8%0.0
7 px x-height90100.0%95.8%92.5%−3.3
9 px x-height30100.0%94.2%98.3%+4.2
9 px x-height60100.0%100.0%100.0%0.0
9 px x-height90100.0%100.0%100.0%0.0

There it is. Aggressive JPEG recompression costs 15–27 points of accuracy, and upscaling recovers part of it — most dramatically at a 7 px x-height and q30, where native reading drops to 73.3% and the upscale brings it back to 89.2%, a 15.8-point gain. At q60 and q90 the gain is zero or negative every time.

The mechanism is the mirror image of section 4. JPEG at q30 has already low-passed the image into 8×8 blocks with ringing at the edges; interpolation on top of that is a second smoothing that happens to suppress the block boundaries the same way a larger kernel would. The upscaler is not recovering text information — it is undoing compression damage. Two low-pass filters in series approximate one better low-pass filter.

So the working rule is narrower than either instinct:

  • Lossless source, small text → upscaling makes it worse. Re-export at a larger size instead.
  • Heavily compressed source, at or above a 7 px x-height → upscaling helps, up to about 16 points at q30.
  • Heavily compressed source, below a 7 px x-height → upscaling cannot help. The information is gone before compression and compression removed more of it.
  • Moderately compressed (q60–q90) → upscaling does nothing measurable either way, so do not spend the compute.

6. Sharpening after the upscale: no

One more reflex worth checking, since it is the standard advice. An unsharp mask at radius 1.0, 80%, zero threshold, applied after a lanczos ×4 upscale:

Source x-heightlanczos ×4+ unsharp maskDifference
5 px81.7%80.8%−0.8
7 px83.3%85.8%+2.5
10 px94.2%93.3%−0.8

Mean effect: +0.3 points. One improved case, two regressions, all within the noise this matcher produces on a 120-character sample. Sharpen because the result looks better to you, not because it reads better — for text at these sizes those are different questions, and unsharp masking raises edge contrast while also amplifying the ringing that creates the c/e confusions in the first place.

7. What this means for a real screenshot

Convert the threshold into things you can see. The x-height to font-size ratio in this font is 0.55, so a 7 px x-height is a 12.7 px font. Whether that is readable depends entirely on the capture:

Capture contextFont size for 7 px x-heightVerdict
72 dpi export12.7 ptmost body text qualifies
96 dpi standard screen9.5 ptborderline; small print fails
144 dpi / HiDPI capture6.4 ptanything 7 pt or larger reads cleanly

Which is the argument for taking the screenshot at 2× device pixel ratio in the first place. The same window captured at 144 dpi instead of 72 dpi has twice the x-height in pixels, crosses the readability line for all its body text, and needs no upscaling at all — and it is a 4× larger file for 4× the pixels, exactly like any other genuine resolution increase.

8. What I did not measure

Three honest limits. The matcher is single-font, so the template count is 36 classes with no case variation and no confusion between serif and sans — a general OCR engine faces a much harder problem, and the absolute percentages here are optimistic for anything but clean digital text. Scanned paper, anti-aliased subpixel rendering on an LCD, and rotated captures are all out of scope. And the JPEG arm used PIL's encoder, which is a decent stand-in for a browser screenshot but not identical to one.

The direction of every result, though, rests on comparisons within a single fixed matcher, where the absolute calibration matters much less than the ordering. Upscaling losing to native reading by 8.7 points on lossless sources did not depend on the matcher being good; it depended on the same matcher reading both arms.

If you are working through a batch of small-text images and need them at a usable size for anything other than reading — a slide, a thumbnail, an asset for a page — the resolution increase still has to come from somewhere. upgolab handles that case, with the caveat this article is about: upscale the picture, but read the text at the size it was captured, or go back and capture it bigger.

Reproducing it

Everything above comes from about 90 lines of numpy and PIL. The matcher, the sweep, the native versus upscaled comparison, the JPEG arm and the sharpening test are each self-contained enough to run on their own. Copy the render, cell_vec, split_cells and read_accuracy helpers, then for each condition render at the source size, upscale with whichever kernel you want to test, and read with the cell pitch multiplied by the factor. Print per condition accuracy and the mean margin — the margin (correct-class score minus best wrong-class score) is the more sensitive signal when accuracy saturates near the 98.3% ceiling and stops discriminating.
